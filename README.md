The following is the documentation of a One Health genomic epidemiology investigation I conducted in collaboration with the North Dakota Veterinary Diagnostics Lab and the North Dakota Department of Agriculture. This is an internal document written for the VDL such that the lab can replicate, maintain, or edit my workflow for any future investigations using these bioinformatics tools. The first part of this document will consist of the data analysis I performed on my specific research topic. The second part will contain notes on using the workflows presented here. All datasets, figures, and workflows can be found in subfolders contained in the main branch of this repository.

INTRODUCTION

In 2022, these departments were tasked with investigating a Salmonella outbreak among exotic cats in a North Dakota zoo— two snow leopards and a Pallas’ cat. They found that all three were carrying a multidrug-resistant strain of Salmonella serovar Newport, REPJJP01, that had also infected animals in two unrelated zoos. This strain is associated with imported beef products from Mexico, though no additional evidence has yet come forth about potential food-source contamination for these animals.

This event alerted the departments to the need for additional AMR surveillance among exotic and domestic animals as a potential risk factor of zoonotic spillover to humans. These kinds of animals receive considerably less attention than commercial food animals such as poultry, swine, and cattle, which are well-established reservoirs of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria and are subject to extensive antimicrobial use in agricultural settings.

The VDL asked me to analyze genomic data from a set of roughly 2600 Salmonella isolates from the FDA’s Veterinary Laboratory Investigation and Response Network (Vet-LIRN), a federal program that monitors animals for AMR pathogens. This dataset had previously been unanalyzed by the department due to constraints on time, staff, and bioinformatics capacity, and the department was interested in documenting unusual AMR genes found in animal reservoirs and in serovars besides Newport.

This analysis was conducted primarily on GalaxyTrakr, a graphical user interface dedicated to public health bioinformatics and pathogen genomics. Below, you'll find my Galaxy pipelines, results, and associated metadata.

1) EXPLORATORY ANALYSIS- Characterizing a rare multidrug-resistant cassette in unexpected hosts 

I first filtered the Vet-LIRN dataset for isolates taken from exotic animals, domestic animals, and wildlife. After organizing these isolates by serovar, I discovered a population of serovar Typhimurium isolates spread among different bird and mammalian species (Dataset 1- Vet-LIRN, "Enterica serovars"). After  retrieving sample accession numbers profiling for AMR genes using metadata from NCBI Pathogen Detection, I discovered a cassette of multidrug-resistant genes (blaCMY-2, sul2, tet(A)) among two domestic cats and a wild turkey (Table 1- Vet-LIRN, "Typhimurium metadata"). 

The blaCMY-2 gene is an antibiotic resistance gene that encodes an AmpC-type beta-lactamase enzyme. It provides bacteria with resistance to penicillins, cephamycins, and third-generation cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone and ceftazidime (Call et al., 2009). It has been associated in the literature with Salmonella Typhimurium and E. coli in commerical poultry farming operations, typically carried by an IncI plasmid designated in MOB as plasmid cluster AA474 (Robertson et al., 2023; Sanderson et al., 2023; Habib et al., 2025). Indeed, IncI and IncX plasmid backbones were detected in the Vet-LIRN isolates, suggesting that a mobile genetic element may have been carrying this gene and others.

2) PHYLOGENETIC ANALYSIS- Limited clonal relatedness between blaCMY-2+ isolates

A contextual analysis of the cat/turkey isolates and 934 poultry-derived Typhimurium isolates within the same NCBI Pathogen Detection SNP cluster showed that blaCMY-2 is rare among these isolates’ immediate clonal relatives, occurring in only 12 (1.3%, Dataset 2- Typhimurium SNP context). Further, the isolates taken from the domestic cats and wild turkey fell on distal nodes of a phylogenetic tree of roughly 150 contextual poultry-derived isolates. This pattern was consistent with a hypothesis of horizontal rather than vertical transmission of resistance genes, although the phylogenetic data alone could not establish the direction or mechanism of transfer (Dataset 3- Typhimurium phylogeny accessions; Figure 1- Typhimurium phylogeny).

3) SNP ANALYSIS- Isolate chromosomal genomes conserved across diverse source types

Pairwise comparisons of blaCMY-2+ chromosomal core genomes at the single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) level revealed two distinct groups of sequences: a closely related group differing by approximately 0–100 SNPs and a substantially divergent group differing by approximately 30,000 SNPs. The closest sequences differed by only 17 SNPs, indicating high sequence similarity within the closely related group (Dataset 4- SNP Distance). This disparity indicates that the blaCMY-2+ isolates represent both closely related and highly divergent bacterial genomic backgrounds, consistent with either possible clonal expansion or horizontal dissemination of the resistance element. SNP distances alone are not sufficient to determine transmission and must be considered alongside other analyses.

4) PLASMID ANALYSIS- Circulation of related AA474 and AA859 plasmids among domestic animals and animal diets

One proposed route of transmission was food-source contamination: Raw or “wet” animal diets may be prepared from Salmonella-infected poultry, or may be contaminated during processing and shipping, which may in turn infect domestic animals and their human caretakers. I filtered the full NCBI Pathogen Detection metadata table for all isolates taken from poultry products, pet food or treats, domestic animals (Felis catus and Canis lupus familiaris), and human clinical specimens, a total of 900 isolates (Dataset 5- NCBI pet food). Genome assemblies were analyzed for quality control using QUAST; assemblies with N50 <50,000 and contigs >100 were removed from the list. The remaining assemblies were subsequently analyzed using staramr and filtered for blaCMY-2, yielding 32 QC-passed, blaCMY-2+ assemblies across all source types (Dataset 6- Pet food QC_blaCMY-2+).

The 32 assemblies were run through MOB-recon, generating plasmid bins and associated plasmid FASTA files. Full list was filtered for plasmid AA474 (carrying blaCMY2) and a frequently co-occurring plasmid cluster, AA859 (carrying Sul2 and tet(A)). Plasmid assemblies were analyzed using k-mer based assembly tool MashTree, showing related AA474 plasmids circulating among dog and dog food-derived isolates, and related AA859 plasmids circulating among cat and cat-food derived isolates (Figure 2- AA474 MashTree, Figure 3- AA859 MashTree). Both of these subclades showed considerable genetic divergence from turkey-derived isolates, suggesting that domestic animals and animal diets may represent a distinct ecological compartment for the circulation of these AMR bacteria.

5) STRUCTURAL ANALYSIS- Conservation of resistance-region synteny across diverse source types

Two plasmids may be genetically related without being structurally identical-- i.e. they may share a set of core genes but the order of those genes in physical space may be different across plasmids. Assessing whether related plasmids may share a common genetic origin requires examining structural conservation, or synteny, across isolates. Because available plasmid assemblies were too fragmented to fully reconstruct the whole plasmid, contigs containing the resistance genes of interest were extracted and compared using Clinker (Dataset 7- 474_859 Contigs, Figure 4- AA474 Clinker, Figure 5- AA859 Clinker). Resistance-determining contigs from both AA474 and AA859 were conserved across poultry, domestic animal, animal diet, and human isolates. This conservation indicates that the resistance-associated regions are maintained across multiple ecological compartments and provides modest supporting evidence for the movement of these genetic elements among hosts, in lieu of whole-plasmid comparisons.

6) BIOMARKER ANALYSIS- Mobile genetic element machinery present in related plasmids

MOB-Typer identified predicted mobility-associated machinery among the resistance-associated plasmids, including relaxases, oriT-associated sequences, and mate-pair formation genes (Dataset 8- 474_859 Biomarkers). The presence of these genetic features indicates potential for bacterial conjugation or mobile transfer, but does not establish that transfer occurred, that the machinery was functional, or that transfer occurred between the isolates examined.

CONCLUSION

Taken together, the data point to the circulation of a contextually rare, multidrug-resistant cassette with modest conservation among distinct ecological settings and hosts, including poultry production, companion animals, animal diets, and human clinical specimens. While no definitive evidence of direct transmission can be inferred from these data, genetic relatedness between isolates and plamids suggest either recent clonal descent or horizontal gene transfer may have occurred, with animal diets potentially acting as an intermediary. The latter mechanism is further suggested by the presence of mobile genetic element machinery among resistance-carrying plasmids. We believe these results justify further epidemiologic surveillance of companion animals and animal feed as under-studied reservoirs of antimicrobial resistant bacteria, with potential transmission to humans remaining an open question.

This project is a pilot study using publicly-available data. As such, limitations on data quality must be taken into account and improved to ensure that future research on this subject can be robust, accurate and predictive. While isolates for all final analyses were quality-controlled, the abundance of short-read sequences and highly fragmented assemblies limited our ability to conduct whole-plasmid analysis, which is needed to more confidently establish conservation of plasmid architecture across transmission events. Time should be dedicated to filtering more strictly for high-quality assemblies, and to improved long-read assembly capacity if possible. In addition, more human isolates should be included in future analyses to assess the risk of transmission to poultry workers and pet owners. Understanding these questions through a One Health lens represents a fruitful strategy for establishing potential transmission chains and communicating preventative action to populations at greatest risk.
